Why juniper matters

Juniper has spread across Oregon's rangelands

Western juniper (Juniperus occidentalis) once grew mostly on rocky ridges and rimrock, where fire seldom reached. Over the last 130 years or so, its range has grown roughly tenfold, from about to around in central and eastern Oregon and adjoining states.

As it spread, vast areas of sagebrush steppe became juniper woodland. That shift changes how water moves through the land, lowers rangeland productivity, and adds to water shortages as the climate grows drier.

The Camp Creek Paired Watershed Study, started in 1994, tests what happens when juniper is removed. Its long record shows lasting gains after the cut: more perennial grass and shrub cover, more herbaceous forage, wetter soil, and more springflow and streamflow.

About the project

Two neighboring watersheds, one cut and one left alone

A paired watershed study watches two similar, side-by-side drainages for years. Then one is treated and the other is not. Because both get the same weather, the difference between them after treatment shows what the treatment did.

At Camp Creek, Mays watershed had nearly all (~90%) of its western juniper cut in 2005. Jensen next door was left as it was. Before the cut, both had about the same number of mature junipers ().

The two watersheds have been continuously monitored for multiple soil, water, and vegetation variables since the beginning of the project in 1994. Learn more at ecohydrology.oregonstate.edu.

A systems approach

From hilltop to aquifer to valley

We study each watershed as one connected system. What grows on the uplands changes how much rain and snowmelt soaks into the soil, how much recharges the shallow and deep aquifers, and how much reaches springs and the stream in the valley.

  • Upland woodlands and the valley below are linked by water moving over and through the ground.
  • Groundwater and surface water feed each other.
  • The same water supports riparian areas, wildlife, livestock and downstream communities.

Where does the water go after junipers are cut?

Green water first, then blue water

Juniper keeps water from reaching the soil in two ways. Its dense canopy catches rain and snow, which then evaporates straight back to the air: on two Jensen plots the canopy caught 36 to 46% of what fell (Ochoa et al. 2018). And as an evergreen, it draws water from the soil whenever conditions allow, not only during the main growing season.

When the juniper is cut, both of those losses stop, and the water that is freed up can take two broad paths.

Green water is water that stays in the soil and is used by plants. It goes first, because the dry soil under juniper acts like a sponge that must fill up before anything moves on. It shows up as wetter soil, more grass, forbs and sagebrush, and more forage. Some of it also returns to the air through the new plants and from the bare, warmer soil.

Blue water is water that moves past the root zone. Once the soil sponge is full, the extra drains deeper to recharge groundwater, feeds springs, and reaches the stream. This happens mostly in normal and wet years, when there is enough water to fill the soil first.

How the freed-up water splits between these paths depends on the year's weather, the soil and geology, how much juniper was removed, and how fast new plants fill in. At Camp Creek we saw both: wetter soil and more grass in every year, and extra streamflow mainly in normal and wet years.

Scaling up

From two small watersheds to much larger ones

The two Camp Creek watersheds total about 524 acres (212 hectares). To learn what juniper management does at much larger scales, the lab has extended the approach to central and eastern Oregon. Clover Creek (Crook County) is about 8 times, and Drewsey (Harney County) about 27 times, the size of the two Camp Creek watersheds combined.

These sites let us estimate the effects of juniper management on streamflow and water use at much larger scales, using single-watershed and nested-watershed approaches (smaller watersheds measured inside larger ones). The projects started in 2023 and build on what we learned at Camp Creek.
